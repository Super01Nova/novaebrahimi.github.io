## Overview

After the pandemic, video conferencing became the default for online classes, while virtual worlds such as Gather.Town, Virbela, Second Life, and Minecraft stayed on the margins. My earlier interviews captured the expert view. This study asked learners who had used both kinds of platform to compare them head to head, so I could find where each one helps and where it gets in the way.

## The problem

Schools usually pick one platform for everything, mostly for convenience. Research tends to evaluate each platform alone, which says little about the real decision teams face: which tool fits which activity. Without the learner's view, product teams and educators end up guessing.

## Research goal

Find out how students' learning experiences differ between virtual worlds and video conferencing, and what each platform's design makes easy or hard.

## Approach

**Segmented groups.** I ran three focus groups, each built around one platform family so participants could build on shared experience: Gather.Town; Virbela and Second Life; and Minecraft. All participants had taken classes on both a virtual world and a video conferencing tool, and they ranged from undergraduates to postgraduate learners across several fields.

**Comparative moderation.** Instead of asking about one platform at a time, I asked students to judge each against the other. Comparison surfaced details neither platform's users would mention on their own, such as how leaving a breakout room actually works.

**Sessions and synthesis.** Sessions ran 70 to 90 minutes on Zoom. I cleaned and de-identified every transcript, then used inductive thematic analysis to let themes come from the students' own accounts. I mapped the resulting themes onto the experience framework from my expert study to see where learners agreed with educators and where they added something new.

## Key insights

### Where virtual worlds win

**Freedom to move changes engagement.** Students were surprised by how much simply walking around mattered. One said that spatial freedom "made a tremendous difference" in how they engaged with the material.

**No waiting for the host.** In video calls, a student who drops out of a breakout room has to wait for the instructor to put them back. In a virtual world, they just walk back to their group's table.

**Chance encounters and natural audio.** Proximity audio let voices fade with distance, so students could run into classmates in a hallway or notice others dancing in the middle of the room. Video calls offer no equivalent: joining someone else's breakout room uninvited felt wrong.

**Time flies.** Students described losing track of hours in Minecraft, while in video lectures they caught themselves "watching the clock."

**Students take the floor.** Anyone could present, share a screen, or build. Avatar customization, usernames, and shared builds let students bring their identity into class and own their work.

### Where video calls win

**Fast to start.** "Just send out a Zoom link" and a group can talk for 20 minutes with no setup or learning curve.

**Focus for lectures.** With nothing to explore, attention goes to the speaker. Some students said they learned "more seriously" on Zoom.

**Accountability.** A visible camera kept some students on task.

**Reliability and reach.** Low system requirements and familiarity made video calls easier to access for everyone.

### The big takeaway

The platforms are complements, not competitors. Each one's strengths cover the other's weak spots, so the useful question is not "which platform?" but "which platform for this activity?"

| Activity | Better fit |
|---|---|
| Lectures and formal presentations | Video call |
| Short check-ins and quick syncs | Video call |
| Long collaborative sessions | Virtual world |
| Creative and building projects | Virtual world |
| Social events and community building | Virtual world |

## Outcome

- Platform-by-activity guidance for educators and institutions choosing tools
- 23 design opportunities, each traced against the expert findings so I could see which needs both groups shared
- One published paper

## Challenges and reflection

**Recruiting was the hardest part.** Finding students with real classroom experience on both platform types took far longer than planned. The same barrier later stopped me from validating a survey instrument I had drafted to measure the framework at scale. If I ran this again, I would build recruitment partnerships with instructors before finalizing the study design, and plan a smaller pilot of the survey in parallel.

**Small, focused sample.** Nine students across four platforms gave depth, not breadth. The findings point to directions worth testing with a larger group.
